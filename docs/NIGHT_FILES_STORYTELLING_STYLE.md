# Night Files storytelling standard (all LLM providers)

This guide is shared across the primary writer, alternate writers, script editor, explainer adaptation, and critics. Follow it regardless of model/provider.

## Core voice
- Sound like a skilled person telling a gripping story to a friend, not an encyclopedia, a lecture, or a sales pitch.
- Open in the middle of the interesting event: the hook must relate directly to the title's central promise.
- Clear, conversational spoken English; precise, concrete imagery; varied sentence lengths; no filler or repetitive phrases.
- Third-person is natural for documented historical cases. Second-person only if it genuinely serves a fictional POV or a carefully chosen immersive telling. Never force “you watch,” “you follow,” or “you witness.”
- The central incident/question belongs in the opening, not the last third. Relevant background belongs only where it explains the incident.
- Each beat advances the story: new evidence, complication, choice, change, consequence, or revelation. Avoid a chronology of loosely related facts.
- Establish real stakes, maintain clear cause-and-effect, and end with a meaningful payoff or an honest unresolved question, not a generic call for comments.

## The viewer-value contract (Night Files, October 26, 2026)
Before writing each new script, define all three privately:
1. **Lesson/discovery:** "After watching, the viewer can explain ..." Name one specific new understanding beyond a creature's name, appearance or powers. A scare alone is insufficient.
2. **Payoff:** Name the supported answer, reveal, consequence or clearly explained uncertainty that rewards the hook within this video.
3. **Call to action:** Choose exactly one relevant action after delivering the lesson and payoff. Invite a meaningful contribution, sharing with a relevant person, or following for a specific related learning promise. Never stack comment/share/follow requests.

Build the hook around the discovery, then use connected evidence, decisions and consequences to earn it. Cut padded adjectives and repeated atmosphere. Move a useful question to the opening when the story can explore it. Never automatically end with "what would you check tonight?" or an unrelated survival question. A CTA cannot replace the ending.

For folklore, research how the legend came to be: earliest documented accounts, cultural context, spread and changes between versions. Choose the strongest supported discovery; do not force an origin claim when sources cannot establish one. Distinguish earliest recorded appearance from an actual origin, and documented context from a proposed explanation. Mark disputed or unknown origins clearly. Attribute escape rules to versions of the legend, never real safety advice.

For historical mysteries, teach what the evidence supports and what remains unknown. For original fiction, earn an insight through the character's decision and consequence, clearly within fiction; never invent a real-world scientific or historical lesson. No forced moral or sermon.

**Editor/critic review:** Identify the exact narration delivering the lesson and the payoff, and the single CTA. Quote a weak line, explain the specific problem, and propose a concrete revision when any requirement is missing. Do not accept a monster description as discovery or a generic final question as payoff. Recheck changed narration within the existing bounded revision budget, without extra model-call loops. Unavailable review is unverified, never a passing result. Subjective editorial scores do not predict retention and do not discard already completed safe videos.

These November 2, 2026 owner requirements supersede older task prompts that make learning optional or demand a viewer question at every ending. Preserve source integrity, existing JSON schemas, owner-approved text and production safety gates.

### Make it instantly understandable
- Establish WHAT happened, WHO or WHAT it affects and WHERE the story occurs as soon as those details help
  comprehension. Never bury the actual storm/disappearance/event beneath an invented agency, a ledger, three
  dates, a desk lamp or scene-setting.
- Explain necessary specialized words in their first spoken use *inside the story*, in ordinary language
  (e.g. say "old sea stories" instead of an unexplained "maritime legends"). If it isn't needed to understand the
  mystery, delete it. Do not turn the explanation into a lecture.
- When the narration promises N clues/reasons, make each item distinct, understandable, and causally connected to
  the same question. Use ordinal cues ("first", "second", "third") if a numbered graphic is warranted.
- Do not invent scientific proof or pretend a technical claim disproves a hoax. An intact seal, faded ink or missing
  weather reading is a **clue inside fiction**, not a proven forensic conclusion. In real stories, every evidentiary
  conclusion requires a source. In fiction, the narrative's own rules must still make cause and effect believable.
- Fictional cases may feel like case files, but never masquerade as independently documented historical events.
- Judge edits by what a normal viewer will **understand, remember or feel**, not by the number of facts, unexplained
  jargon, eerie props, visuals or transitions.

## Retention writing rules, owner directive October 27, 2026

These are editorial decisions, **not claims that viewership increased or that a new scheduler exists**. They extend rather than replace the viewer-value contract and the existing five-part tension arc. Use only the techniques that honestly fit the story.

1. **R1, a specific opening problem.** Start inside a concrete event, consequence, contradiction, question or decision that matches the title and the opening picture. No atmospheric preamble, generic "you won't believe," empty shock, biography-first setup or obligatory channel intro.
2. **R2, connected mini-loops.** Within the main narrative arc, when useful, develop a small question/problem, a meaningful investigation/attempt/progression, and an answer or consequence that alters the situation. Then connect the next question to what was just learned. Do not delay every answer until the end. No arbitrary "twist every N seconds" requirement.
3. **R3, escalating consequences.** A new fact or complication must change what a character can do, what the audience knows, which explanation is plausible, what is at risk, or what an earlier clue means. Avoid interchangeable scary images and manufactured setbacks.
4. **R4, give the viewer a legitimate role.** In selected original fictional survival narratives, second person or a meaningful viewer choice can help when stakes and possible actions are understandable. Never force "you watch," audience participation, or choices into nonfiction; don't fabricate votes or have viewer choices determine scripted historical facts.
5. **R5, honor the hook's promise.** Every short delivers a real payoff, specific consequence, honest answer, or well-framed uncertainty in its own runtime. A cliffhanger may open the next episode, but must not replace the current episode's earned result. No "follow for part two" bait.
6. **R6, continuity across shots and episodes.** Maintain consistent identities, locations, props, rules, knowledge, injuries, time/order and decisions when they matter. Reuse relevant imagery and source/approved assets; show changed objects/actions instead of unrelated generic horror atmosphere.
7. **R7, visuals prove narrative progress.** Each beat's principal image, real clip, evidence item or restrained graphic should correspond to the narrated action/claim. Hard cut by default; add motion, captions, sounds, time labels or a progress indicator only when meaningful. Preserve the one-second cover, character approval, rights, caption safe zones and the **at-most-one grounded date timeline per video** rule.
8. **R8, original repeatable formats.** A stable premise, recognizable visual packaging and consistent question can help viewers know what kind of outcome to expect without repeating a plot. For cultural or horror-character commentary, original comparison/analysis may refer to recognizable works and relevant excerpts when justified; verify source, rights, accurate claims and platform limitations case by case rather than treating all such commentary as automatically forbidden or automatically cleared.
9. **R9, one trackable central question.** Define the question, what changes the viewer's understanding, and what the final evidence/outcome supports. When relevant, a single minimal in-scene motif, clue progression, or verified counter can make that progress clear. No invented numerical scores, legal verdicts, charges, evidence, milestones, or decorative scoreboards. A sourced mystery may end unresolved.

**Narration-first review check:** Could a first-time viewer state (a) what the problem is, (b) what new information changed it, (c) what was answered, and (d) what remains unknown? Is the second conflict genuinely connected? Does the visual at each consequential line show the object/action/evidence and not merely atmospheric wallpaper? Rewrite only for specific problems, never to meet an artificial twist quota.

### Occasional original three-episode fictional horror

- **Format approved, individual concepts/scripts and their production releases are NOT pre-approved.** Maintain standalone Night Files stories as the default and never silently turn every upload into a series. Keep a coherent common setting, characters, important objects, fictional rules and consequence ledger/series bible. Each of three episodes must stand alone enough for a newcomer, deliver an earned local payoff, and build toward one resolved or honestly unsettling trilogy conclusion. No automatic fourth episode.
- **Series-start editorial frequency:** begin no more than one new trilogy per seven full days **AND** per 14 *confirmed, actually published* Night Files posts since the prior trilogy began, whichever threshold takes longer. All three trilogy posts count toward the total. Drafts, buffer entries, failed upload attempts, retries, test videos and pending queue items are not confirmed publications. If history is incomplete or a previous trilogy is unfinished, hold eligibility for review.
- **This is a writing/approval constraint, not an implemented rotation, posting scheduler or proof of live analytics.** Before series generation/publication, inspect the current history and existing queue/checkpoint system, obtain the necessary owner approvals and add a verified fail-closed eligibility guard inside the maintained bot. Do not manually dispatch production workflows, move buffered videos, bypass approvals or create parallel publishing systems merely to test a story.
- Do not copy other creators' stories, names, art, scenes, series titles, dialogue, characters, or proprietary visual branding. These competitors illustrate storytelling techniques, not reusable assets or proven causes of view counts.

## Rule R9: Give the viewer a question they can track (owner, October 27, 2026)

**For every Night Files script**, identify privately during writing and review:
1. **Central question:** What specific question, problem or promise can a viewer follow from the hook to the ending?
2. **Changed understanding:** Which real event, observed action, verified clue, counterexample, decision or consequence actually changes what the viewer understands while watching?
3. **Payoff:** What answer, consequence, revelation or honest uncertainty does the ending deliver in response to the original question?

Make progression intelligible **through the primary cinematic footage and narration first**. When the particular story benefits, allow **one understated, story-grounded recurring visual motif or evidence indicator** (for example, a single familiar key, radio signal indicator or documented progress label) to help track meaningful changes. A motif is optional, not an instruction to put a counter on every video or repeat a card at every beat.

**Source and state discipline:** Every displayed number, charge, status, clue, metric, comparison, counter, verdict and conclusion must be supported by what the narration establishes, and in nonfiction, reliable documentary evidence. Fictional UI must be clearly part of the fictional story, not presented as factual reporting or a proven forensic conclusion. No fabricated charges, invented verdicts, manufactured scoreboard, arbitrary percent progress, unexplained numerals, or unsupported red/green status boxes.

**Editing discipline:** Preserve Night Files' cinematic primary imagery, semantic shot matching, meaningful hard cuts, minimal evidence-based graphics, nonlinear easing only for justified motion, accurate one-time documentary date timeline, synchronized readable captions, approved one-second cover, and existing FFmpeg/Remotion editor. R9 requires **clear narrative cause and effect**, not a new animation engine, overlay quota, or separate narrator.

**Review gate:** Can a viewer say the question, the fact/choice that changed their understanding and the answer or justified uncertainty at the end? If not, fix the story and visual match rather than inventing a tracking graphic.

## Owner story-craft addendum: eight priorities, November 6, 2026

These **eight story-writing requirements** refine R1–R9 and the five-part arc. They apply to full original horror and sci-fi short scripts, and where appropriate to nonfiction mystery/folklore. Use them as quality goals, not a rigid eight-scene or effects checklist. Write like a compelling story first, then adapt its visuals.

1. **Relatable character:** Give the central person a recognizable ordinary need, vulnerability, responsibility, desire, insecurity or difficult relationship that directly affects their choices. Establish this economically through a natural action or concrete detail, not an unrelated biography. Readers should understand *why this person cares*. In sourced nonfiction, never invent someone's thoughts, motivations or relationships.
2. **Meaningful events:** Every major event changes a decision, danger, relationship, clue, possibility or consequence. Connect cause and effect, so characters' actions matter. Cut scenes and details that merely repeat the same atmosphere or plot information.
3. **Strong hook, meaningful re-hook, earned hook payoff:** The opening makes a specific promise; a later development genuinely reinterprets or complicates it; the ending fulfills that original promise. The re-hook must be connected to the first problem and should not be mechanical, time-based or fake. Check the hook and payoff against the *entire final script*.
4. **Fear of mystery and possibility:** Build dread through uncertainty about **what could plausibly happen next**, the unseen danger, incomplete knowledge, hard choices and the consequences of getting it wrong. Maintain enough clear rules and real stakes that uncertainty feels thrilling instead of confusing. Show the threat's effects when stronger than revealing the threat itself. No forced jump scares.
5. **Every second and detail earns its place:** Especially in a roughly one-minute short, each spoken line, pause, object, behavior, sound and visual beat must add character, understanding, tension, cause or payoff. Keep precise, memorable sensory/physical details that matter later; remove redundant opening setup, repetitive reveals and filler. Clarity and a deliberately held moment of dread still count as value. Do not cram every second with exposition or motion.
6. **Dialogue is optional:** Use it only where a brief exchange, deception, argument, warning, choice or distinct voice makes the scene stronger than narration alone. Dialogue must sound like human speech, reveal intent or conflict, and change the scene. A well-told narrated story does not require dialogue.
7. **Surprise beyond the expected, but earned:** Seek an ending that exceeds or overturns a reasonable viewer prediction by changing the meaning of an earlier clue, decision or relationship. Seed fair clues and preserve consistent character/world logic. A random new monster, unexplained rule, contradictory revelation or unsupported real-world claim is not a clever twist. For genuine unresolved cases, an honest surprising insight or perspective beats a fabricated solution.
8. **Complete first draft before full review:** Write the **entire script**, including its beginning, connected developments, reveal/climax, resolution and ending, **before** handing it to the script editor, challenger, scorer or review stage for a full-story verdict. Do not call a partial opening, outline or unfinished ending a reviewed script. Planning, owner concept approval and essential safety/source checks may happen earlier. After the complete draft exists, use the existing bounded editor/critic process, make at most the already-authorized targeted revision, and verify the **final revised complete script** again. Do not add uncontrolled model calls, bypass required approvals or defer writing the ending until rendering.

**Final writer check:** Why does the audience care about this person? What decision causes the next event? What exactly is promised in the first seconds? Where does the promise change meaning? What concrete danger might happen? Which detail matters later? Is dialogue genuinely needed? What makes the ending both unexpected and inevitable in hindsight? Has the whole script been written before review?

## Cascading suspense loops: stakes, big question, head fake, re-hook (owner, November 7, 2026)

Use this **four-step storytelling lens** inside the existing R1–R9 rules, five-part arc, eight story-craft priorities and complete-script-first review. It describes connected audience curiosity, **not a medically proven “dopamine addiction loop”** or a promise of casino-like effects. Apply it where the material earns it, not as an obligatory four-scene template.

1. **Stakes:** Make viewers care about a specific person and outcome. Establish **a relatable character, something personally meaningful at risk, and urgency when real or narratively justified**. Stakes need not involve death; a threatened responsibility, trust, livelihood, safety, answer or relationship can matter. Show them through action or precise verified context, not invented inner thoughts, fabricated deadlines or melodrama.
2. **Big question:** From those stakes, make **one concrete central question** irresistible and intelligible. Give enough context for an audience to predict what might happen, without dumping exposition or promising an answer the evidence cannot supply. This feeds R9's trackable question and the main hook.
3. **Head fake / expectation reversal:** Let the story's established facts or character choices suggest **A**, then reveal a supported **B** that changes what the viewer reasonably believed. This can be a genuine contradiction, new clue, changed interpretation or consequence, not an arbitrary jump scare or a narrator falsely implying A is proven. **In nonfiction, never invent a discovery, character reaction, motive, chronology, threat or historical conclusion merely to create a twist.** If no legitimate reversal exists, use an honest refinement of the question or skip the head fake.
4. **Re-hook:** Let B immediately create the **next meaningful, causally connected question, risk or decision**. Answer enough of the preceding question to reward attention; do not pile up mysteries or conceal every payoff. The next loop must grow from the previous evidence or consequence, not from a generic “but then” or unrelated cliffhanger.

**Cascade rather than repeat:** A strong standalone script can have one main loop and, where justified, smaller **stakes → question → evidence-based reversal → connected re-hook** cycles. The ending must still discharge the original hook's promise with a real answer, human consequence or clearly bounded uncertainty. Do not demand a reversal or re-hook every fixed number of seconds. Every spoken detail must advance character, evidence, understanding, danger or payoff.

**Example, documented Flannan Isles lighthouse case:** The keepers were missing when relief arrived (**stakes and question: where are the three men?**). The orderly station contrasts with severe damage at the western landing (**supported expectation shift**). That evidence changes the question to what happened near the water (**re-hook**). Investigators considered a large wave the likely explanation; their final moments remain unobserved (**honest payoff, not a supernatural claim**). Do not invent thoughts for Joseph Moore or imply the wave was witnessed.

**Writing and review sequence:** Establish the four beats during planning as useful, **finish the entire narration and ending first**, then have the existing bounded editor/critic/scorer identify the lead and risk, central question, the exact line of any supported expectation shift, what new question it causes, and the final payoff. A missing manufactured twist is **not** a defect. Compare actual completed video retention only when data exists; a writing score is not evidence of addiction or guaranteed retention.

## Retention repair, owner review November 6, 2026

Apply these checks to the complete spoken narration, including scene boundaries:
- Open once. After the hook, advance the event instead of restating its sentence, date, setting or premise. Do not repeat the opening at the end to manufacture a playback loop. If a historical task requires "This is a true story.", use it once and continue with new information; never use it for folklore or fiction.
- Deliver useful information early. The next one or two beats must introduce a sourced clue, explanation, decision or consequence that changes understanding. Do not spend the first half listing appearance, powers or atmosphere and save every discovery for the last line. No invented evidence to satisfy this timing.
- Make the lesson specific. For folklore, investigate who first recorded it, how it circulated, the cultural context and differences between versions. A country name, creature description or list of powers alone does not fulfill the discovery. Distinguish a first surviving record from the unknown creator/origin; if the supplied sources cannot establish origins, teach a different supported discovery and state the limit.
- Earn the ending before the CTA. Answer the opening promise or explain exactly what remains unknown. Replace generic questions such as "What would you check first?" with one invitation grounded in the discovery. For a legend comparison, a suitable example is "Does your hometown have a version? Tell us its name and where you heard it." Vary the invitation to fit the story; do not repeat this exact template in every video. Treat viewer replies as research leads, not verified facts.
- During the existing editor/critic pass, quote the early discovery, payoff and CTA, and identify repeated opening information across scenes. Repair concrete defects within the existing revision budget. Do not add model calls or regenerate safe completed videos because of subjective scores.

Analytics are diagnostic context, not a writing formula: average view duration is not an exact exit point; one high-view story does not prove a genre wins. Compare matched video lengths, retention curves and engaged-view definitions before claiming an improvement.

## Adaptation by genre
- Unsolved mystery: the disappearance/event first, then necessary context, evidence, search, competing sourced interpretations, and what remains unknown.
- Historical event: defining moment first, then how/why it unfolded and why it mattered.
- True crime: incident, people, facts, evidence, documented outcome; never invent accusations or motives.
- Folklore: tell the legend as a coherent escalating tale while marking supernatural claims as belief or lore.
- Fictional horror: a clear unsettling premise, escalating consequences, consistent internal rules, and earned ending.

## Truth and trust
- Preserve established names, dates, quotations and events. Never invent facts, quotes, dialogue, certainty, sources or dramatic explanations for real cases.
- Clearly separate verified facts, witness claims, interpretations, and unresolved theories.
- The title, hook, scenes and ending must all tell the SAME story. A biography cannot pass as an unsolved mystery merely by mentioning the mystery at the end.
- If a rewrite changes narration, critique the final narration again; never claim an earlier score validates a changed script.

## Saying what kind of claim it is
- Fact: said plainly ("Earhart and Noonan never reached Howland Island" only if the source says so).
- Witness claim: "a radio operator said he heard...". Theory: "one theory is...", "investigators suspected...".
- Rumor or legend: "people whispered...", "the story goes...". Never upgrade a theory or rumor into a fact.
- Fiction never claims to be true, and true stories never borrow fiction's invented certainty.

## Endings
- Mystery: end on what the evidence actually shows and the leading explanations, then the honest open question.
- Solved case / historical event: end on the documented outcome and why it matters.
- Legend: end on the legend's own ending or what people still believe. Fiction: an earned, understandable ending.

## How quality is checked (bounded, owner rule Oct 24)
- Code measures concrete problems (title subject / event late or missing, biography before the event, stage
  directions like "you watch", repetition, numbers not in the source, fiction presented as fact).
- One primary draft, at most one challenger draft, one independent comparison, at most one targeted revision.
- A low subjective score guides the revision and the analytics; it never throws away a finished, safe video. Only
  demonstrable serious problems block (fabricated numbers/years, off-title story, fiction told as fact).

## Editor and critic checklist
- Reject a title/story-promise mismatch, irrelevant biography/filler, fabricated detail, repetitive setup, weak scene causality, unexplained jump, misleading hook, or empty payoff.
- Do not give high scores to a script that mostly avoids its stated subject. Explain concrete weaknesses and revisions, not just a number.
- Never treat missing/unavailable critique as a passing grade.

## Examples of the intended direction (not scripts to repeat)
- Weak for a mystery: “She was born in 1897 and loved climbing trees...”
- Stronger: “Amelia Earhart and Fred Noonan vanished over the Pacific in 1937. What do we actually know about their last flight?”
- Weak: “You watch as she boards the plane.”
- Stronger: “Then the flight reached the stretch of ocean where contact was lost.”

This guide is writing guidance, not source evidence. Never use it to add facts to a story.
