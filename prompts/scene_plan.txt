# Night Files scene plan instructions.
# Step 2: turns the finished story script into scenes, image prompts, sounds and the caption.
# {script} and {sfx_list} are filled in by the bot. Edit anything else freely.

You turn a finished horror narration script into a scene plan for a vertical TikTok video.

SCRIPT:
"""
{script}
"""

SPLIT IT INTO SCENES
- Split the script, in order, into 7 to 12 scenes of 1-3 sentences each, and put each part in "narration".
- COPY THE WORDS EXACTLY. Do not rewrite, shorten, add or reorder anything. Only remove markdown symbols, headings and quotation marks around dialogue if they would sound odd read aloud.
- Scene 1 must be the script's opening hook.

IMAGE PROMPTS
- Every image prompt is a full cinematic description of 25 to 45 words: WHO is in it (by character name) and what they are doing, the exact setting details, the lighting (lamp glow, moonlight, flashlight beam, shadows), the camera angle and shot size, and the mood. Short prompts like "the narrator looking shocked" produce bland images and are not allowed.
  Good example: "Medium shot of the narrator gripping a chipped yellow coffee mug at a worn diner counter at 5 AM, harsh fluorescent light, rain on the dark window behind her, the bald stranger's reflection in the glass, tense and uneasy"
- People look like real adults with realistic proportions (not cute, not childlike, no big cartoon eyes). Never draw children unless the story has a child character.
- TWO images per scene: "image_prompt" = the main visual of what the narration says (when the words say "the bridge", show the bridge); "image_prompt_2" = a genuinely DIFFERENT visual from the same narration: another subject, action or angle (e.g. the key object in close-up, the place as a wide shot, another person's reaction). Never the same picture twice. Leave "image_prompt_3" and "image_prompt_4" empty (and their image_location fields "none").
- Illustrated storybook / animated-film look, NOT a photo. Show the characters clearly with EXPRESSIVE FACES (worry, fear, confusion, relief). Faces sell the emotion.
- REAL PEOPLE (only when the script is about real people): shown with normal visible faces in this same illustrated style, matching only basic public facts (approximate age, hair, clothing, era). Do NOT try to copy a real private person's actual face. Historical figures (dead 100+ years) may follow known portraits. Masked or hooded figures are fine when the story fits (thieves, disguises). Each real person gets a "characters" entry with a fixed look reused word-for-word in every shot they appear in.
- ONE FRAME ONLY: Every image is ONE continuous full frame, like a single frame from a film. Never comic panels, page layouts, storyboards, collages, split screens or multiple versions of a scene.
- NO READABLE TEXT: never ask for readable text, words, signs with lettering, neon lettering, title cards, headlines, or documents/screens/phones with words on them (the image AI turns them into garbled fake text). Show those objects without legible text: "a blank hotel sign glowing red", "a laptop screen glowing in the dark".
- MEDIA SOURCE per shot: "image_source" (and "image_source_2".."_4") is "ai", "stock_video" or "real_photo", and "image_query" (and "_2".."_4") is a 2-5 word search query for that shot. real_photo = TRUE STORIES ONLY: a real place, building, vehicle, object, document or official sketch/wanted poster that exists in public archives (query = its real name, e.g. "Cecil Hotel Los Angeles", "Boeing 727"); NEVER private people, victims, bodies or crime-scene photos. stock_video = generic atmosphere with NO story character in it (rain on a window at night, fog between pine trees, a flickering hallway light, a city street at night). ai = every shot with a story character or a specific story moment. The first shot of scene 1 is always "ai". At most 3 real_photo shots and about 40% stock_video shots per video; fiction never uses real_photo.
- TRUE STORY VISUALS (only when the script is about real events): for real stories, keep objects, clothing, vehicles, buildings and era historically accurate. If the source doesn't establish a visual detail, keep it generic.
- Write each image prompt in this order: the exact action or event, the main subject, the key object or environment, then framing (e.g. "medium shot"), lighting, mood. What to draw comes before how to draw it; no flowery sentences.
- PER-SHOT LOCATION: every image prompt has its own location field: "image_location", "image_location_2", "image_location_3", "image_location_4" = the exact "locations" name where THAT shot takes place, or "none" (object close-ups, sky, anything not in a listed place). An outdoor shot (forest, river, road, sky) never uses an indoor location like a cabin; if the story goes outside, add that outdoor place to "locations".
- OBJECT SHOTS: in a close-up of a thing (a note, a briefcase, money), the object is the subject: describe the object and the surface it lies on, nothing else; its location is "none".
- Write every image prompt as plain descriptive text, never labels like "SHOT:" or "SETTING:". The main subject + action + key object come in the first 12 words; then framing, lighting, mood.
- Every shot in the video is a different picture: never two prompts with nearly the same subject and framing.
- CONSISTENCY IS EVERYTHING: viewers must recognise the same people and the same rooms from shot to shot. Every "characters" and "locations" entry has a SHORT fixed "look" (max 20 words, concrete: age, hair, clothing) that is never changed.
  - "characters": list every character once with a short "name" (e.g. "the night guard", "the old neighbor") and a fixed "look" (age, build, hair, exact clothes and colors, one prop; appearance only, never emotions or expressions), e.g. "a young man, mid 20s, short black hair, grey work jacket, flashlight".
  - "locations": list the 1-3 places the story happens with a short "name" (e.g. "the living room") and a fixed "look" (layout, furniture, colors, key objects), e.g. "cozy suburban living room, beige couch, old CRT TV on a wooden stand, tall lamp, staircase in the back".
  - In every image prompt, refer to characters by their exact name ("the night guard shines his flashlight at the old neighbor"). Their look is added automatically, so don't repeat it. Set each scene's "location" to its main location name, and each image's own "image_location" field (see PER-SHOT LOCATION).
  - Keep the same outfits and the same rooms for the whole story unless the story truly moves.
- VARIETY, like a film: only about HALF the shots show a character. The rest are establishing shots of the place, key objects and clues, traces of the threat (a shape in the fog, an open door, footprints), and atmosphere. Characters stay consistent when they appear; they don't need to appear in every frame.
- KEEP EVERY SHOT SIMPLE AND DRAWABLE: one clear subject doing one clear thing, seen from a normal angle. The image AI draws these badly, so never ask for: isolated body parts (boots, feet, hands) without the whole person, extreme low or high angles, people seen from behind or over the shoulder (the image AI puts faces on the back of heads and twists bodies; show people facing the camera or in side profile instead), mirror reflections that differ from reality, "two shadows", or anything that only makes sense with a caption. Show the moment before or the reaction instead (e.g. the person staring at the mirror in shock, not the impossible reflection).
- Lighting tells the story: warm, cozy lamp light at the start; darker, colder and more shadowy as the tension rises; darkest at the climax.
- Keep the threat mostly hidden until the climax: a shadow, a shape under the bed, a hand, eyes in the dark. Show it clearly at most once.
- No text or writing in the image. No blood or gore. Nobody is ever shown hurt.
- No all-seeing eye, eye-in-a-triangle or Illuminati-style symbols in any image, UNLESS the story itself is about that topic (e.g. a video about the Illuminati or the eye on the dollar bill). Eyes in the dark are fine.
- Scene 1's image must be the most striking, eerie image of the whole story (it is the thumbnail).

SOUND EFFECTS
- A sound only goes on a scene when the narration of THAT scene literally mentions the sound or its source (footsteps, breathing, a whisper, a knock, a door, a phone, a scream, a heartbeat). Otherwise use "none".
- Match the surface and place exactly (footsteps on pine needles = footsteps_leaves; in a hallway = footsteps_hallway).
- 2 to 4 sounds per video at most. Never on scene 1 (the hook). Silence is scarier than a random sound.
- Allowed sounds: {sfx_list}

OTHER FIELDS
- title: 3-7 word internal title.
- premise: one sentence summary of the plot.
- setting: the main place, in a few words (e.g. "hospital basement night shift").
- threat: what the danger really is and what it wants, in one short sentence.
- twist: the key reveal / ending concept, in one short sentence.
- hook_overlay: 2-5 word on-screen text shown big over the first seconds. It must be shocking on its own, not a title, e.g. "It texted from my phone", "I don't have a landlord", "He waved from under the ice". No emojis.
- twist_scene: the 0-based index of the scene where the twist hits.
- caption: line 1 = the exact phrase people type into search for this story (e.g. "What happened to D.B. Cooper?", "What is the Wendigo?"); the channel adds "illustrated horror story" itself. Line 2 = a question for comments. Max 150 characters. May use 1 emoji.
- hashtags: 5 topic hashtags without # (the case, place, legend or subject + its genre, e.g. dbcooper, unsolvedmysteries). The channel adds #illustratedhorror #horrorstory itself. Never fyp, foryou or viral.
- pinned_comment: a short comment the creator can pin to start discussion.
